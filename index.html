North Carolina · 1939–1956

Two Journeys on the
Same Southern Roads

Travel guides, segregation, and the freedom to move.

Five historical objects · An ENGL 102 exhibition

01

Introduction

A highway can connect two places without giving everyone the same welcome along the way. In North Carolina, automobile tourism promised mountain scenery, seaside recreation, and the freedom to choose a destination. For Black travelers living under Jim Crow, a trip also required knowing where they could eat, buy fuel, and spend the night. This exhibit asks: How did mainstream travel guides and the Green Book portray automobile travel through the Jim Crow South, and what do their different emphases reveal about racial discrimination and travelers’ freedom of movement?

Jim Crow was a system of racial segregation and unequal treatment maintained through laws, institutions, and everyday practices. Its consequences extended into ordinary travel decisions. Driving a private car could give Black motorists greater control over their journeys than segregated public transportation, but reaching a town did not ensure access to its services. Scholar Isabel Dorothea Kalous explains how the Green Book addressed this problem by circulating practical knowledge among African American travelers. The guide made movement more manageable within a discriminatory environment; it did not remove that environment.1

North Carolina’s 1939 Variety Vacationland brochure encouraged people to visit throughout the year, with photographs of activities for every season. The Federal Writers’ Project’s state guide, published the same year, gave practical information about transportation, lodging, and recreation. The Asheville page shown here reported nineteen hotels, including two for Black travelers, but gave no names or addresses for those two hotels. The Green Book gave readers names and street addresses to help them plan their stops. For a Black motorist, deciding where to go also meant finding places along the way that would serve them.

The Green Book’s directory listed businesses and tourist homes serving Black travelers. Its North Carolina listings turn cities into networks of possible stops. A later advertisement for Payne’s Tourist Home in Wilmington pairs a house photograph with promises of comfort and convenience. Geographer Ethan Bottone interprets tourist homes as spaces where hospitality could resist exclusion and support Black mobility. This perspective helps us see these addresses as evidence of community work and enterprise, rather than treating Black travel only as a story of restriction.2

The five objects move from tourism’s promise to its practical limits, then to resources travelers used to navigate those limits. They include a photographic collage, two textual guide pages, a photographic advertisement, and a highway map. The 1939 and 1941 sources offer closely spaced perspectives; the Fall 1956 advertisement provides a later example, not proof that travel conditions steadily improved. A listing cannot guarantee safety, and an omitted business cannot establish exclusion. Historical racial terminology remains visible in the original scans. For classmates familiar with Southern roads but new to this history, the exhibit’s purpose is to make freedom of movement concrete: access depended on both roads and the people and places available along them.

02

Five objects

1A year-round tourism calendar 2An Asheville accommodation guide 3A North Carolina travel directory 4A tourist-home advertisement 5A 1939 highway map

Object 1

A vacation for every month

Date: 1939

Creator: North Carolina Department of Conservation and Development, Advertising Division

Media: Photographic tourism collage

This calendar transforms North Carolina into twelve opportunities for recreation. Photographs of skiing, fishing, swimming, and other activities arrange the state around a visitor’s leisure rather than a resident’s everyday work. The slogan “Every Month a Vacation Month” makes enjoyment seem continually available. Repeated monthly panels encourage readers to imagine choosing when to visit, with the landscape ready to accommodate their plans.3

That persuasive design leaves an important practical question unanswered on this spread: which travelers could actually use the services supporting these vacations? The photographs cannot establish admission rules or the experiences of people outside the frame. Their significance lies in the promise they construct. Placed before the guidebook’s accommodation categories and the Green Book’s addresses, the collage invites us to distinguish a destination’s appealing image from the unequal conditions under which people reached and experienced it.

Object 2

Segregation in the practical information

Date: 1939

Creator: Federal Writers’ Project for the State of North Carolina

Media: Textual guidebook page

Asheville’s guidebook entry places lodging beside bus fares, golf fees, swimming locations, and annual events. Within this ordinary planning material, it reports nineteen hotels, with two designated for Black travelers. It also separates two of eight motion-picture houses by race. These parenthetical distinctions show racial division being recorded as a routine feature of the visitor’s environment, rather than explained as a problem requiring change.4

The page complicates any claim that mainstream guides simply ignored Black travelers. It acknowledges them, but mainly through categories and counts. Unlike the Green Book page that follows, it does not name those two hotels or provide their addresses. That difference affects what a reader can do with the information. The figures are the guide’s published account, not a complete independent census of access policies. Their strongest evidence is rhetorical: segregation appears embedded in an otherwise abundant catalogue of urban convenience and recreation.

Object 3

Addresses that make a journey possible

Date: 1941

Creator: William H. Green, editor; Victor H. Green, publisher

Media: Textual travel directory

The North Carolina section replaces broad promises with names and addresses. Asheville’s listings identify the Y.W.C.A. at 360 College Street and the Palace Grill at 19 Eagle Street. Durham’s categories extend beyond lodging to beauty parlors and service stations, while Greensboro’s entries repeatedly point readers toward East Market Street. The compact city headings and business categories make the page usable while planning a stop.5

Kalous’s study helps explain the importance of this format: the Green Book circulated knowledge that Black motorists needed to navigate segregation.6 Its directory presents travel as a sequence of practical needs, supported by local places and people. It also reveals a broader experience than merely finding a bed; travelers might need food, grooming, fuel, or social space. These listings document what the publication recommended in 1941. They neither guarantee a visitor’s safety nor prove that every unlisted business refused Black customers.

Object 4

A house presented as hospitality

Date: Fall 1956

Creator: Payne’s Tourist Home; published by Victor H. Green & Company

Media: Photographic advertisement

Payne’s Tourist Home advertises more than an available room. Its photograph shows a substantial house with a porch; the accompanying text emphasizes comfort, convenience, and steam heat. An address, telephone number, and proprietor’s name, C. F. Payne, turn that invitation into information a traveler could act on. Compared with the directory’s compressed lines, this display advertisement gives hospitality a visible setting and an individual provider.7

Bottone’s research interprets tourist homes as places where hosting could support Black mobility and resist exclusion.8 That argument helps illuminate the work behind a listing: someone had to provide the welcome that a road alone could not supply. The ad also appeals to travelers as customers entitled to comfort, not merely as people avoiding danger. Its language remains an advertiser’s claim, rather than independent evidence of every guest’s experience. As a later object, it shows how the Green Book’s practical network could also market desirable accommodations.

Object 5

Connected roads, unequal access

Date: 1939

Creator: North Carolina State Highway and Public Works Commission

Media: Highway map excerpt

The western portion of this highway map joins towns, forests, and the Great Smoky Mountains National Park through an organized road network. Its colored lines and route numbers make the region appear navigable: a motorist can trace connections between a city and a recreational landscape. The map supports the physical possibility of travel, but it does not identify which restaurants, lodgings, or other services would receive Black motorists.9

That limitation brings the exhibit’s objects together. The vacation calendar makes destinations desirable; the Asheville guide records racial distinctions; the Green Book supplies usable contacts; and Payne’s advertisement presents a place to stay. Each answers a different planning question. The boxed numbers visible here were added by the modern archive and are not part of the original 1939 map. We are viewing historical road evidence, not reconstructing a particular traveler’s itinerary. Freedom of movement depended on more than the connections a map could draw.
