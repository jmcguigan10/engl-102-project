Two Journeys on the

Travel guides, segregation, and the freedom to move.

Five historical objects · An ENGL 102 exhibition

01

Introduction

North Carolina’s tourism publications invited visitors to explore mountain scenery, beaches, and outdoor recreation. Black motorists also needed to plan where they could eat, buy fuel, and sleep. Jim Crow shaped access to these everyday services. A business could refuse a Black customer because of their race. Travelers relied on advice about places that would receive them. This exhibit asks: How did mainstream travel guides and the Green Book describe the choices available to motorists traveling through the Jim Crow South? The five objects here focus on North Carolina between 1939 and 1956.

Jim Crow enforced racial separation through laws, business practices, and social customs. Discrimination followed Black travelers as they moved from town to town. People who could afford a car could choose their own route. They planned stops for food, fuel, and lodging. Scholar Isabel Dorothea Kalous explains how the Green Book helped travelers prepare for these needs. Publisher Victor H. Green collected information about businesses serving Black customers. He gathered recommendations through other postal workers. Publishing this information made local knowledge available to people planning a trip.1

North Carolina’s 1939 Variety Vacationland brochure promoted travel in every season. Its calendar page showed activities ranging from skiing to swimming. The Federal Writers’ Project published its state guide in the same year. The guide gave details about transportation, lodging, and recreation. Its Asheville page reported nineteen hotels. Two were designated for Black travelers. These racial categories appeared alongside bus fares and golf fees. The page presented segregation as part of the information a visitor needed to plan a stay.

The Green Book gave readers business names and street addresses. Its North Carolina listings included hotels, restaurants, beauty parlors, and service stations. Tourist homes offered another place to stay. People rented rooms in their homes to travelers. The Fall 1956 advertisement for Payne’s Tourist Home in Wilmington shows one business presenting its accommodations to readers. Its photograph and description emphasize comfort and convenience. Geographer Ethan Bottone argues that hospitality in tourist homes supported Black mobility and resistance to exclusion. The people who opened these homes helped travelers find a welcome in unfamiliar places.2

The exhibit begins with the vacation calendar and the Asheville guide page. The 1941 directory then shows how travelers could locate businesses. Payne’s advertisement gives a closer view of one tourist home in 1956. A 1939 highway map places this travel in the landscape of western North Carolina. Each object connects a part of trip planning to life under segregation. The exhibit invites classmates to consider the work behind an ordinary journey. Choosing a destination meant arranging meals, fuel, and a place to sleep. Black travelers depended on local businesses and shared knowledge as they made those plans.

02

Five objects

1A year-round tourism calendar 2An Asheville accommodation guide 3A North Carolina travel directory 4A tourist-home advertisement 5A 1939 highway map

Object 1

A vacation for every month

Date: 1939

Creator: North Carolina Department of Conservation and Development, Advertising Division

Media: Photographic tourism collage

This calendar presents North Carolina as a place for recreation throughout the year. Twelve panels pair each month with a photograph. Skiing, fishing, swimming, and sailing give readers different ways to picture a visit. The slogan “Every Month a Vacation Month” ties the images together. Its repeated format makes a trip seem easy to fit into a reader’s plans.3

The collage emphasizes the visitor’s leisure. The photographs show people enjoying outdoor activities. The state becomes a setting for their vacations. This image of tourism invites readers to choose a destination and a season. The guide pages that follow add details of where visitors could stay and find services. Seen together, these sources connect the desire to travel with the practical work of choosing a place to stop.

Object 2

Planning a stay in Asheville

Date: 1939

Creator: Federal Writers’ Project for the State of North Carolina

Media: Textual guidebook page

The Asheville page groups lodging with bus fares, golf fees, swimming locations, and annual events. It reports nineteen hotels and marks two for Black travelers. The page also counts eight movie theaters. It designates two for Black audiences. These details appear in parentheses within a page of everyday travel information. Their placement presents racial separation as an ordinary part of planning a visit.4

A hotel count tells a reader how the guide divides accommodations. A name and street address help a traveler choose a specific stop. The directory shown next supplies those details. Reading the pages together shows how segregation shaped the information a visitor needed. A drive to Asheville included decisions about where to sleep, eat, and spend time.

Object 3

The Green Book’s North Carolina listings

Date: 1941

Creator: William H. Green, editor; Victor H. Green, publisher

Media: Textual travel directory

The 1941 Green Book organizes North Carolina businesses by city and type of service. Asheville’s entries name the Y.W.C.A. at 360 College Street and the Palace Grill at 19 Eagle Street. Durham’s listings include beauty parlors and service stations. Several Greensboro entries direct readers to East Market Street. The short headings and street addresses help a traveler look up services for a planned stop.5

Kalous explains how the Green Book shared knowledge Black motorists used to navigate segregation.6 The categories on this page show the range of services involved in a trip. Food, lodging, grooming, and fuel all depended on local providers. The directory connects those needs to specific businesses and tourist homes. Its published recommendations give readers a set of contacts to consult as they plan their journeys.

Object 4

Payne’s Tourist Home, Wilmington

Date: Fall 1956

Creator: Payne’s Tourist Home; published by Victor H. Green & Company

Media: Photographic advertisement

This 1956 advertisement introduces Payne’s Tourist Home to Green Book readers. A photograph shows a large house with a porch. The text advertises comfort, convenience, and steam heat. It supplies the address, a telephone number, and the proprietor’s name, C. F. Payne. These details help travelers picture a stay. They also show how to contact the proprietor.7

Bottone connects tourist-home hospitality with Black mobility and resistance to exclusion.8 Payne’s name gives a personal point of contact. The address and telephone number show readers how to reach the home. Its photograph and sales language encourage readers to expect a pleasant stay. The Green Book’s directory identifies services by category and location. This advertisement gives readers a picture of one home and the welcome its proprietor advertises.

Object 5

Roads through western North Carolina

Date: 1939

Creator: North Carolina State Highway and Public Works Commission

Media: Highway map excerpt

This 1939 highway map connects western North Carolina’s towns, forests, and the Great Smoky Mountains National Park. Colored lines and route numbers organize the roads across the region. A reader can trace a route from a town to a park. The vacation calendar encourages readers to imagine a trip. The map helps them locate roads and destinations. Route numbers give travelers a way to identify highways on a planned drive.9

The five objects describe different parts of that planning. The calendar encourages a visit through photographs of leisure. The Asheville guide divides hotels into racial categories. The Green Book names services and gives their addresses. Payne’s advertisement presents a particular place to stay. Together, these sources show how a journey depended on roads, local knowledge, and people offering services. For Black motorists, those everyday choices took place within a segregated travel system.
